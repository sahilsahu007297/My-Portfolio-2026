## The current app: what a visitor can see

The public Aarthik app is now available. After a browser-local email entry and an introduction, the demo opens an overview with an available balance, a daily safe-to-spend amount, subscriptions, transactions and cards. It offers Overview, Earnings, Assistant, Payments and Insights in its current navigation. These screens have evolved from the earlier Home, Insights, Add, Learn and Profile structure documented above.

The overview makes the variable-income direction visible with “Every way you earn” and “Track daily wages, gigs & projects.” The inspected Insights view includes cash flow, spending and budget entry points, with sample behavioural summaries. The displayed balances and insights are demo content, not evidence of connected bank accounts, executed payments or verified personalised financial analysis. The original architecture and research account above describes the design process; it should not be read as a release checklist for the current demo.

## Monetisation: charge for useful planning, not financial anxiety

The model I would test for Aarthik is a consumer subscription. The product's design centres on irregular income, a daily spending estimate and planning around uneven payments. A subscription would need to earn its place through repeated usefulness, not through the promise of guaranteed financial improvement.

This is a commercial proposal. The public app demonstrates the product experience; the inspected screens do not establish a live paywall, payment collection, bank connectivity or paying customers. I have not assigned a launch price or forecast revenue from screens alone.

I would first test whether people return to the basic income-and-obligations view and understand how its spending estimate is calculated. A possible free tier would support a useful basic view. A possible paid tier could add longer planning horizons, scenario comparisons and richer history, if those capabilities are built and people demonstrate a need for them. These tier boundaries are proposals, not an inventory of existing app features.

The explanation behind a spending estimate should remain accessible. Someone should not have to pay to discover which bills or income assumptions produced a number they are being asked to trust. Missing data and uncertain future income should be visible, regardless of tier.

I would defer lending referrals, investment commissions and a voucher marketplace as launch revenue sources. They are not established commercial arrangements in this project. They could also pull the experience away from helping someone judge a purchase toward encouraging another financial transaction.

## How Aarthik could become a sustainable business

The immediate user value is less work reconstructing a financial picture and a clearer distinction between money received and money available after commitments. For a freelancer, that could support day-to-day cash planning. It does not make Aarthik an accounting, tax-filing, invoicing or payroll product.

The business hypothesis is that a useful recurring planning task can support a recurring subscription. I would measure whether users return to the planning view through both high-income and low-income periods, whether the inputs stay current, and whether people can explain the output correctly. Opening the app frequently is not enough if it leaves someone more confused.

A paid pilot would test willingness to pay after people have used the relevant features. I would track trial-to-paid conversion, renewal, cancellations and refunds alongside complaints about incorrect or misunderstood estimates. An attractive price shown in a prototype is not payment evidence.

At the operating level, contribution per paid subscriber would be subscription revenue excluding taxes and refunds, less payment processing, data access, hosting and attributable support costs. The cost of supporting free users also has to be covered by the business. Bank-data or partner fees, if integrations are introduced, belong in that calculation rather than being treated as free infrastructure.

Acquisition payback would depend on observed contribution and renewal, not an assumed lifetime value. I would not publish a payback period before there is enough paid usage to estimate it. A simpler product that people trust and renew may be more viable than a broad feature set with costly integrations.

## What must be true before this model is sold

The account-aggregator, payment, authentication and infrastructure choices in the original study are proposed architecture. They are not verified integrations in the available app evidence. Provider names identify design options, not partnerships or proof that every listed operation is supported.

Likewise, the research and prototype-testing figures earlier in this study come from the supplied design document. The representative SUS response is not a production satisfaction score. Neither those figures nor this commercial model establish revenue, reduced financial stress or improved saving behaviour.

Before charging for an automated spending estimate, I would validate the calculation against known inputs, show how stale or missing data affects it, and make it possible to correct assumptions. Before charging for a planning feature, I would verify that the feature is present and dependable in the paid version. The accurate description today is an interactive finance demo with a proposed subscription model; a live commercial service still needs validation.
